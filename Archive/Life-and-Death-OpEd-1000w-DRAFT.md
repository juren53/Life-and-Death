# Learning the Meaning of Life and Death

### A trimmed op-ed version (~1,000 words) — for LA Times oped@latimes.com submission

*Draft prepared 2026-07-28*

---

by Jim U'Ren

This September 17 marks the 60th anniversary of a plane crash that happened a few hundred feet from my front door in North Hollywood — and of an act of split-second sacrifice that I am still, six decades later, trying to fully understand.

In the summer of 1966, I was thirteen years old and a Dodger fan with a devotion that bordered on the maniacal. Late-season games carried a weight that was hard to account for rationally but impossible to deny emotionally. We used to joke that it wasn't a matter of life and death — it was much more important than that. I did not yet know how wrong I was.

That Saturday afternoon, September 17, I was in our house on Goodland Avenue, listening to the Dodger game on the radio. We lived close enough to Burbank Airport that aircraft noise was just background hum — you stopped hearing it after a while. What broke the pattern that afternoon was a sound completely out of the ordinary: a low, deep-throated, menacing growl, growing too loud and too fast, asymmetric and urgent in a way normal aircraft sounds never were.

Then the house went dark. The shadow of the plane swept over us, total and sudden, blotting out the sun. I froze in front of the radio. The roar peaked, the shadow slid off the far side of the house, and the sunlight came flooding back. Then came the explosion — not a sharp crack but a rolling concussion that shook the house for several seconds. Shouts. Running feet. Mothers calling for their children.

A plane had crashed at the end of our street. I rushed outside to flames, billowing smoke, and secondary explosions, and my thirteen-year-old brain came slowly to the gut-wrenching conclusion that someone had just lost their life in that inferno.

The aircraft was a North American AJ-1 Savage — a twin-engine Korean War-era naval bomber converted into a wildfire tanker, operated out of Burbank. It had just taken off, fully loaded, when it lost an engine — a pilot's nightmare with no good outcome available, only a choice between bad ones.

The pilot was Major John Francis "Jack" Hennessy, 47, a decorated veteran of two wars. He'd served in World War II, survived Korea, come home, raised a family, and kept flying — fighting the wildfires of California in place of the enemies he'd once fought overseas. In the final seconds of his last flight, with his plane going down over houses full of families on a Saturday afternoon, he made a decision. He banked his wounded aircraft and turned it away from the neighborhood, trading his last chance at survival for ours. The maneuver cost him his life. He is buried at Forest Lawn in Glendale. His headstone reads simply: *Major, 729 Bomb Sq AF, World War II, Korea.* It deserves more: *Here lies a man who saved countless lives as he lost his own.*

What I did not learn until years later was how close this story had already come to my family. My father's oldest friend, a Disney pilot named Jim Bissell, was also a World War II veteran pilot — he and Jack Hennessy had known each other since the war, survived it, and both went on to fly professionally out of Burbank. Jim and his wife Betty were fixtures of my childhood, "Uncle Jim" and "Aunt Betty," friends of my parents since their 1946 wedding. The man who died at the end of our street had been my adopted uncle's old friend from the war. That night, my mother cut my father off at the dinner table before he could say more — she'd had enough for one day — and it would be years before I understood the full shape of the coincidence.

I never got to ask Uncle Jim about his friend. I wanted to know what it was like to fly with him, what kind of man he was, how you come home from something like that and live an ordinary life. But I was a kid, my parents steered me away from the questions, and my family moved to St. Louis two years later. The Bissells sent Christmas cards for years afterward. The conversation I wanted to have never happened — the way so many conversations don't, when you're young enough to assume there will always be more time.

Years later, I learned to fly myself, training out of Burbank, soloing off the same runway Jack Hennessy had departed from on his last flight. Going through my preflight checklists, I would hear a voice in the back of my head that could have been my instructor, or could have been Hennessy: stay focused, don't hurry, don't take shortcuts. What I came to understand, more fully once I was flying myself, was the precision of what he'd done in those final seconds. He had not merely tried to avoid the houses. He had succeeded — engineering a controlled outcome from an uncontrollable situation, choosing deliberately in a moment when most people have nothing left but panic.

None of us on Goodland Avenue ever got to thank him. None of us even knew his name that day.

The Dodgers won their game that afternoon, and went on to the World Series, but something in me had changed. The pennant race that had seemed, that afternoon, like the most consequential thing in the world had been placed, without my consent, next to something else — and in that comparison it found its actual size. That is what a genuine brush with death does. It does not ask permission to rearrange your sense of proportion. It simply does it, quietly and permanently.

I am still a Dodger fan, sixty years on, and pennant races still sometimes feel like life and death. But a pilot named Jack Hennessy, in the seconds he had left to him over a Southern California neighborhood, taught a thirteen-year-old boy what life and death actually means.

---

*Jim U'Ren is a Los Angeles native and licensed pilot who trained at Burbank Airport.*
